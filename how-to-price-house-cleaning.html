Residential cleaning pricing guide

How to price a house cleaning job without guessing

There is no universal “correct” cleaning price. A useful quote starts with the time and costs you expect, then adds enough room for the business—not just the job—to work.

Fast version: estimate total labor hours, value those hours, add supplies and travel, allow for overhead, then apply a target margin. Inspect the scope before sending the final quote.

1. Estimate total labor hours

Use total team hours. If two people work for three hours, the quote must support six labor hours. Include setup, packing, and other predictable on-site time. Use your completed-job history when you have it; memory usually favors the smooth jobs.

2. Put a value on labor

Your labor target is not automatically your take-home pay. Depending on how your business is structured, the quote may also need to support payroll costs, insurance, non-billable admin time, cancellations, and taxes. Use your real obligations and professional advice where needed.

3. Add costs that change with the job

Common direct costs include consumable supplies, laundry, parking, tolls, disposal, and special products. Equipment replacement and software are often easier to handle in an overhead allowance than as a line item on every quote.

4. Add overhead before profit

Overhead is what keeps the business operating even when it is not inside a client’s home: insurance, bookkeeping, software, equipment, training, marketing, and unpaid admin time. If the quote covers only labor and chemicals, those costs have nowhere to go.

5. Choose margin deliberately

Markup and margin are different. Adding 20% to a $100 cost produces a $120 price and a 16.7% margin. To target a 20% margin, divide the cost by 0.80, which produces $125.

Target quote = (labor + supplies + travel) × (1 + overhead rate) ÷ (1 − target margin)

The result is a planning number, not a command. Review the home’s condition, requested scope, access, pets, timing, risk, local market, and your minimum viable job price before sending it.

6. Separate standard work from extras

Ambiguous scope creates callbacks and lost time. Name what a standard clean includes, then separately confirm work that changes duration: ovens, refrigerators, interior windows, inside cabinets, heavy buildup, post-construction dust, excessive pet hair, laundry, or organization.

7. Improve with completed-job data

After each job, record estimated hours versus actual hours and any unplanned costs. A simple monthly review is more valuable than copying another cleaner’s price sheet because it reflects your speed, service area, and operating costs.
