Worked example / use your own rates

A 3-bedroom, 2-bath cleaning quote, explained line by line.

In this fictional example, a $210 quote covers $166.75 of entered costs and leaves $43.25 before any costs we have not included. Here is where those numbers come from—and how one extra hour changes them.

This is not a market-rate recommendation. A bedroom count alone cannot determine a cleaning price. All costs, hours, and margin targets below are invented to explain the method. Inspect the home and replace them with your own assumptions.

1. Set a clear scope before choosing a number

Imagine a maintained home needing kitchen surfaces, two bathrooms, dusting, vacuuming, and mopping. Inside the oven, inside the fridge, interior windows, and heavy buildup are excluded. The cleaner estimates four total labor hours. Two cleaners working two hours would use the same four labor hours.

A larger home, difficult access, pets, or a different condition could require more time. Confirm that scope with the customer before relying on the estimate.

2. Build the cost floor

  • Labor: 4 hours × $30 = $120. The hourly figure should account for your own labor or relevant employment costs.
  • Supplies: $15 allocated to the job.
  • Travel and parking: $10 allocated to the job.
  • Direct cost: $120 + $15 + $10 = $145.
  • Overhead: 15% of $145 = $21.75. This is a chosen allocation, not an industry standard.
  • Cost floor: $145 + $21.75 = $166.75.

Overhead can represent equipment, insurance, software, or administration. Do not add a cost again if you already included it elsewhere.

3. Apply margin, then review the rounded quote

For an example 20% target margin, divide the cost floor by 0.80. Simply adding 20% would be a markup and would produce a different margin.

$166.75 ÷ (1 − 0.20) = $208.4375 → $208.44

If this cleaner instead proposes $210 before sales tax, the buffer is $210 − $166.75 = $43.25. The resulting margin is $43.25 ÷ $210 = 20.6%. Check the final price after any discount, because a discount also reduces the buffer.

4. See what happens if the job takes an extra hour

Keep supplies and travel fixed, but increase labor to five hours. Labor becomes $150; direct cost becomes $175; the same 15% overhead allocation becomes $26.25. The revised cost floor is $201.25.

At the unchanged $210 price, just $8.75 remains, or a 4.2% margin. A 20% target margin would now require $251.56. This is why recording actual labor time matters more than copying another cleaner's price list.

5. Turn the checked price into a clear proposal

State the included work, separate extras, frequency, price, and any assumptions that need confirmation. The CalmDeskKits quote tutorial demonstrates a separate offline proposal tool using fictional details. That tool uses the rates you set; it does not inspect the home or choose your local market price.

Before sending a proposal, use the free scope checklist and review every amount. Sales tax collected for a government is not job revenue for this margin calculation. This example does not cover every tax, insurance, payroll, or payment-processing obligation.
